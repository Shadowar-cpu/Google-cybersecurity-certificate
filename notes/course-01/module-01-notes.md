## What cybersecurity means

Cybersecurity means protecting systems, networks and data from attacks and unauthorised access.

If an attacker steals information, changes important data or stops a service from working, it can affect people and organisations. Security work helps protect that information and keep the work running.

These are the foundations introduced in the [course overview](https://www.coursera.org/learn/foundations-of-cybersecurity).

## What a cybersecurity analyst does

An analyst checks security information and looks into suspicious activity. If something looks wrong, they need to investigate it and report what they find.

The work can include:

- Checking alerts and records of system activity.
- Looking for information that explains what happened.
- Writing down findings clearly.
- Reporting a concern to the right person or team.
- Helping the organisation find and reduce security risks.

An alert does not automatically mean an attacker got in. The team needs to check the evidence before deciding what happened. These duties relate to the defensive cybersecurity and incident response roles in the [NICE Framework](https://niccs.cisa.gov/workforce-development/nice-framework).

## Threat, vulnerability and risk

These words are connected, but they mean different things.

| Word | Simple meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Threat | Something that could cause harm. | Someone trying to steal an account. |
| Vulnerability | A weakness that could be used. | An easy-to-guess password. |
| Risk | The chance of harm and how serious it could be. | The account being taken over and its data being exposed. |

An attacker can try to use a weakness. The risk also depends on what they could reach and what protection is already there.

These are short revision explanations based on [FEMA’s threat and vulnerability terms](https://emilms.fema.gov/is_0860c/groups/126.html) and [NIST’s explanation of risk](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/risk).

## Other words to remember

**Asset:** Something valuable that needs protecting. It can be a device, an account, important information or a service. The [CISA glossary](https://niccs.cisa.gov/about-niccs/glossary) explains assets in this wider sense.

**Malware:** Harmful software or code. It can steal information, damage files or interrupt a system. Viruses and worms are examples in the [NIST malware glossary](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/malware).

**PII:** Personally identifiable information. This can identify a person by itself or when combined with other details. Information linked to a person, such as their medical or financial records, also needs careful protection. [NIST explains PII and its context](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/personally_identifiable_information).

**Security posture:** The overall security position of a system or organisation. A stronger posture means less exposure to security risk, as explained by the [Australian Cyber Security Centre](https://www.cyber.gov.au/glossary/security-posture).

## Phishing and Spot the Phish

Phishing is when someone uses a convincing message to get a person to share information, open a harmful link or download something harmful.

The message can pretend to come from a trusted company or person.

Things to check:

- The actual sender address, not only the name shown.
- Where a link really goes.
- Pressure to act immediately.
- Requests for passwords or sensitive information.
- Attachments that were not expected.

Correct spelling does not prove that a message is genuine. The sender and the request still need checking. These checks follow [NIST’s phishing guidance](https://www.nist.gov/itl/smallbusinesscyber/guidance-topic/phishing) and the [FBI’s advice on spoofing and phishing](https://www.fbi.gov/how-we-can-help-you/common-frauds-and-scams/spoofing-and-phishing).

### Practice example

An employee gets a message saying their work account will be blocked in ten minutes unless they log in through a link.

The message is putting pressure on them to act quickly. The employee can open the company’s normal login page or contact support through a known contact method to check the request. This is the independent verification approach described in the [NIST phishing guide](https://www.nist.gov/itl/smallbusinesscyber/guidance-topic/phishing).

This is a made-up example for revision.

## Skills used in cybersecurity

Technical knowledge matters. How a person checks information and works with others matters too.

- **Communication:** Explain the problem clearly so another person can understand it.
- **Attention to detail:** Notice small changes and check whether they matter.
- **Problem solving:** Compare information and work through possible explanations.
- **Teamwork:** Share useful findings and involve the right people.
- **Willingness to learn:** Keep improving as systems and threats change.

Technical skills include understanding systems and networks, using security tools and reading security information. The [Module 1 outline](https://www.coursera.org/learn/foundations-of-cybersecurity) introduces both transferable and technical skills.

## Why cybersecurity matters

An attack can affect more than one computer. It can expose people’s information, stop staff from doing their work and interrupt services that customers depend on.
